# Professional services and implementation

Identify deliverables, scope, dependencies, staffing, schedule, change control, acceptance, payment, expenses and IP. Distinguish an outcome commitment from a reasonable-care obligation; the distinction can matter to remedies and carveouts.

- Make acceptance measurable against agreed specifications. Check review period, rejection detail, cure/retest, deemed acceptance and payment linkage. Neither silence-as-acceptance nor unlimited rejection is universally appropriate.
- Address customer dependencies explicitly: access, data, decisions and personnel. Specify how verified delay affects schedule and cost; avoid allowing unilateral scope expansion through vague cooperation duties.
- Check whether a change order needs authorized agreement before work and fees change. An operational email is not necessarily the required contractual approval.
- Separate termination for cause and convenience, accrued fees, noncancelable commitments, prepaid amounts and transition deliverables. Do not infer entitlement to all remaining fees from a nonrefundable-deposit clause.
- Ownership of a deliverable may be unusable without a license to embedded background materials. Check sublicensing to maintainers, modifications, duration and post-termination use.
- Subcontractor responsibility and personnel-replacement rights should match the actual delivery model. “All staff must be employees” may be an operational mismatch rather than a drafting ambiguity.
- Delay credits/liquidated damages, warranty cure, replacement costs, exclusive remedies and caps require a combined review. A clause labeled “penalty” does not settle its legal treatment.

For a jurisdiction-specific enforceability conclusion, research applicable authority. The English-law Triple Point decision is a useful interaction example, not a universal software-contract rule; see [sources](../sources.md).
