# Incident notice

Use when a draft changes who must notify whom of a security or data incident, what event triggers notice, when the clock starts, how fast notice is due, or who notifies individuals and regulators.

## Read first
- The full notice sentence before and after the edit: event (defined Security Incident, personal-data breach, "suspected" or "potential" incident), scope qualifier (affecting the customer's data, or any incident anywhere in the service), clock start (discovery, awareness, confirmation, determination), deadline, recipient, content and update duties. A strike-through often removes the scope qualifier along with the old deadline; read what survives, not only what is inserted.
- The defined terms: what counts as a Security Incident, whether "suspected" or "discovery" is defined, and whether an unsuccessful attempt (scans, blocked logins) is excluded. Undefined "discovery" can mean the first automated alert.
- Roles and data: who is controller/owner and who is processor/service provider for the affected data, which data classes are in scope (PHI, payment, financial, children's, government), and which regimes apply to each party. Customer-side regulatory deadlines are usually the reason for the ask.
- What a missed or late notice breaches, and where that breach lands in the liability scheme: general cap, an increased-cap category keyed to the security or data section, an uncapped carveout, or an indemnity. See [liability triggers](liability-triggers.md) and [liability and remedies](../issues/liability-and-remedies.md).
- Operational evidence: how the incident-response team actually triages, how long confirmation typically takes, and whether a 24/7 on-call can meet the proposed clock. Ask the security owner rather than assuming.

## What drives the decision
Routine, if within policy: shortening the deadline for a confirmed incident affecting the customer's data (72 to 48 hours, for example); adding a duty to give rolling updates, a named contact and described content as information becomes available; cooperation and reasonable assistance with the customer's own notices; a defined, high-threshold "reasonably believes" standard when the agreement defines it and policy permits.

Risk-changing: (1) notice of *suspected* incidents, or a clock that runs from *discovery* rather than confirmation. Most alerts are not incidents; a hard deadline from first alert forces premature, often wrong notices, burdens the response team during the critical window, and makes the clock start unknowable after the fact, so compliance is litigable. (2) Deleting "affecting Customer data", which turns the duty into notice of any incident on the platform, including other customers' incidents the provider may be obliged to keep confidential. (3) The provider notifying individuals or regulators on the customer's behalf. The controller/data owner usually holds that legal duty and the facts it depends on (who is affected, their residence, which regimes apply, what to say); a processor that sends notices assumes the controller's legal judgment, error risk and regulator contact without the controller's information. Offer assistance, information and, where policy permits, cost allocation ([data breach costs](data-breach-costs.md)) instead.

The decision usually turns on the liability link, not the hours. A tighter deadline is a narrow operational promise if a miss is a general-cap claim; if the increased-cap category covers "any breach" of the security section, every late or misjudged notice of a suspected event becomes a higher-tier claim. Price the deadline after you know which tier it lands in, and fix the trigger or the deadline, not both blindly.

Do not transplant statutory deadlines. A regulator deadline that runs from the controller's awareness (for example the GDPR controller-to-authority period) is not a processor-to-controller deadline; US state breach laws and HIPAA generally put individual/regulator notice on the data owner or covered entity, with the service provider or business associate notifying it. Sector rules (financial, insurance, critical infrastructure, securities disclosure) vary by role and change; research the regime that actually applies to these parties and data before agreeing that a short clock is "required".

Facts that reverse the answer: a customer regulator rule that genuinely needs vendor input within a short period (support a defined, narrow early-warning notice for a stated incident class, not all suspicions); the represented party acting as controller or owner of the data; PHI or another regulated class in scope (a sector agreement such as a BAA may govern); an increased-liability category limited to culpable breaches, which lowers the cost of a tighter clock; a customer-side review, where the same analysis argues for a defined early-warning trigger and committed cooperation rather than notice of every suspicion.

## Common positions
Negotiating context only, not the user's policy. Provider forms commonly give notice "without undue delay" and often within 48 to 72 hours after confirming an incident affecting customer data; regulated customers often ask for 24 to 48 hours and sometimes for notice of suspected incidents. Providers commonly resist suspected-incident notice or confine it to a defined, high-confidence threshold, and commonly decline to send notices to individuals or regulators, offering assistance and information instead. Cost reimbursement for customer notices is a separate negotiated item.

## Worked example
Packet: provider CSA §4 requires notice of a Security Incident affecting Customer Protected Data within 72 hours of confirmation. At renewal ($260,000 trailing fees, US healthcare staffing customer, no PHI in scope) the customer strikes "affecting Customer Protected Data within 72 hours of confirmation" and inserts "or suspected Security Incident within 24 hours of discovery" and "shall notify affected individuals and regulators on Customer's behalf". This deal's §11(b) increased-cap category is any breach of §4 (5x trailing fees); §11(c) excludes consequential damages except indemnity. Policy: notice may shorten to 48 hours after confirmation of an incident affecting Customer Protected Data; no suspected-incident duty; Customer as controller gives notices to individuals and regulators, Provider assists.

Decision: the suspected/discovery change drives the decision. Because §11(b) covers any breach of §4, a missed 24-hour notice of a suspected event would move the claim from the $260,000 general cap to a $1,300,000 cap (5 x $260,000), for an event that may never have been an incident, with no defined clock start; the strike also widens notice to incidents not affecting this customer's data. Counter (within policy): notice within 48 hours after confirmation of a Security Incident affecting Customer Protected Data, keeping the scope qualifier, plus rolling updates. Reject provider-sent notices; offer reasonable assistance and the information Customer needs for its own notices. Note that a 48-hour concession still sits inside the any-breach §11(b) trigger; recheck that category ([liability triggers](liability-triggers.md)). Reversal: a confirmed regulatory requirement on the customer for earlier vendor notice (escalate a narrow defined early warning, outside policy), PHI in scope (BAA terms and HIPAA roles govern), or §11(b) narrowed to gross negligence or willful misconduct (the tighter clock costs less). If the customer insists on suspected-incident notice, that is outside the agreement owner's authority: escalate.
