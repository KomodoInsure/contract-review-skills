# Assignment and change of control

Scope: anti-assignment clauses, change-of-control (CoC) consent, termination or other rights triggered by an acquisition, competitor-acquirer carve-outs and the refund mechanics that come with them, from either the provider or the customer side.

## Read first
- The full assignment clause: who is restricted, the exceptions (affiliates, successors in merger or asset sale), whether the exception is mutual, whether "assignment" is defined to include CoC or assignment by operation of law, and what happens to a non-compliant assignment (void, breach, termination right).
- Every other place that keys off ownership: termination rights, definitions of "Affiliate" and "competitor", confidentiality, exclusivity, most-favored pricing, data-location or export-control commitments.
- Billing and term: annual or multi-year prepayment, invoicing cadence, the termination effective date, and what refund the text actually computes (prepaid fees for a period, all fees, fees for undelivered service).
- Whether "change of control" is defined. Undefined, it may arguably reach equity financings, secondary sales, IPOs or internal reorganizations; venture-backed providers raise rounds that can shift voting control.
- The represented party's position: provider (preserving exit value and freedom to transact) or customer (protecting data, continuity and competitive position after a sale).

## What drives the decision
Separate three asks that often arrive together. Notice of a CoC or assignment, and a successor's written assumption of the agreement, are routine and cheap. A termination right limited to a defined bad outcome (acquisition by a direct competitor of the customer, a sanctioned acquirer, or a successor that will not assume obligations), with a pro-rata refund of unused prepaid fees, addresses the customer's real concern and is a common compromise. A consent requirement on any CoC, or a termination right on any CoC with a full refund, changes risk materially: each such contract becomes a holdout an acquirer must clear, a consent can be withheld to extract price or term concessions, and refund exposure across the customer base reduces deal value or becomes a closing condition. For a venture-backed provider, acquisition is a likely exit, so these rights are priced against the company, not just the contract.

Deleting a mutual successor exception also binds the party that asked for the deletion; point that out, because customers often do not intend to need provider consent for their own reorganizations. Whether a merger is an assignment absent express language, and whether a reverse triangular merger or stock sale triggers a plain anti-assignment clause, varies by jurisdiction, deal structure and contract type (IP licenses are treated distinctly in some jurisdictions). Do not state those outcomes as settled; express language removes most of the uncertainty either way.

Refund math matters. "Refund of all prepaid fees" is ambiguous: it can mean the current period's prepayment or every fee prepaid since signing, and it gives back fees for service already delivered. Draft "prepaid fees attributable to the period after the termination effective date" and fix when termination takes effect, because a 90-day election window can shrink the unused period.

Facts that change the answer: the customer is regulated or government and must vet ownership (foreign ownership, export control, sanctions, sector licensing); the service holds the customer's competitively sensitive data and a specific competitor is a realistic acquirer; the provider is in or near an active sale process (escalate; new consent rights may need disclosure); the deal is strategic enough that the business owner chooses to grant a consent or broader exit. On the customer side, the concern is often not ownership but continuity: an acquirer sunsetting the product. Product-continuity or end-of-life notice, data export and transition assistance, and price protection can address that more directly than consent (see [SaaS](../contract-types/saas.md) on exit).

## Common positions (negotiating context, not policy)
Provider paper commonly permits assignment without consent to a successor in a merger, acquisition or sale of substantially all assets, often mutually, with notice. Customers commonly counter with: successor assumes obligations in writing; no assignment to a direct competitor or sanctioned party; a termination right within 30–90 days after notice of acquisition by a direct competitor, with a pro-rata refund of prepaid unused fees. Consent "not to be unreasonably withheld" for non-M&A assignments is common. Consent on every CoC and full refunds are uncommon outside large strategic or regulated buyers. Post-closing notice is usual because pre-closing disclosure is often restricted.

## Worked example
Packet: new three-year enterprise agreement, $200,000 per year invoiced annually in advance; provider is venture-backed. Redline deletes §14.2's successor exception and adds: any CoC of Provider is an assignment requiring Customer's prior written consent; Customer may terminate within 90 days after any CoC and receive a refund of all prepaid fees. Policy: keep the successor exception and no CoC consent; fallback is a 90-day termination right after notice of acquisition by a direct competitor of Customer, with a refund of prepaid fees for the unused part of the term.

Decision: reject the deletion and the consent requirement (outside policy; they give one customer a veto over a sale and also strip the customer's own successor exception). Counter the termination right with the fallback: trigger limited to acquisition by a direct competitor, with "direct competitor" and "change of control" defined (excluding equity financings, IPO and internal reorganizations), 90 days after Provider's notice, pro-rata refund. Refund if the CoC occurs six months into a contract year and the customer terminates at once: fallback refunds the unused half of the current prepayment, $100,000; the redline refunds $200,000 in year one (a $100,000 difference, six months of free service), and if "all prepaid fees" is read cumulatively, $400,000 in year two or $600,000 in year three ($300,000 or $500,000 more than the fallback). Under the fallback a customer that waits until day 90 receives about $50,000. Reverses if: the customer shows a regulatory need to approve ownership (escalate to the business owner rather than drafting consent), or the business owner approves broader rights for this deal. Anything beyond the fallback escalates to VP Sales.
