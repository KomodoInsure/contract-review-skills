# Liability triggers

Use when a draft adds, edits or removes a category that moves claims out of the general cap: increased-cap or supercap categories, uncapped carveouts, or exceptions to a damages exclusion keyed to a type of conduct or breach.

## Read first
- The general cap and each higher tier: amount, fee base, lookback, anchor, aggregate or per-claim, and whether a higher tier replaces or stacks on the general cap. Compute each tier in dollars from deal facts; see [liability and remedies](../issues/liability-and-remedies.md).
- The existing trigger list, including qualifiers already attached (for example "resulting from gross negligence or willful misconduct"). A new broad trigger can make an existing qualifier meaningless.
- What the trigger references: a numbered section, a defined term, a standard of care, a body of law, or statements outside the agreement. Resolve the defined terms and check the warranty disclaimer and entire-agreement clause.
- Whether the damages exclusion has its own exceptions; a cap trigger does not by itself unlock excluded losses ([damages exclusion carveouts](damages-exclusion-carveouts.md)).
- The approved tier amounts and trigger rule, and insurance limits as a reference point, not as proof of coverage.

## What drives the decision
Read triggers before amounts. A higher multiple prices an agreed risk; a new trigger decides which claims get that price. Ask of each category: could the counterparty plead most ordinary contract claims into it? If yes, it swallows the general cap and the real effect is to move routine exposure up to the higher tier.

Usually measurable and routine, if the amount is approved: breach of a specific numbered section or defined obligation (security section, confidentiality section, a notice deadline, DPA processing instructions); payment obligations; indemnity obligations already defined by claim type. Removing a culpability qualifier from a defined section (from "gross negligence breach of §4" to "any breach of §4") widens the trigger but stays measurable; treat it as an amount-and-scope question.

Cap-swallowing or indeterminate: "negligence" or "negligent performance" (most service failures can be pleaded as negligent, and an ordinary-negligence trigger also overrides any gross-negligence qualifier on the narrower categories); "violation of applicable law" without naming the laws or tying them to a contractual compliance duty (reaches regulatory theories, the counterparty's own regulatory exposure, and any law touching the service); "failure to perform" or "material breach" generally (the whole agreement becomes the higher tier); "misrepresentation" or "inaccuracy of the Service" (imports sales statements and collides with the disclaimer and entire-agreement clause); "breach of the Agreement" disguised as a list; triggers keyed to outcomes or expectations rather than obligations. Gross negligence, willful misconduct and fraud are narrower standards; they are common as carveouts but still depend on applicable law and the approved trigger rule.

Facts that reverse the answer: a broad term defined narrowly elsewhere (for example "applicable law" limited to named data-protection laws governing Provider's processing, which can be tied to a DPA obligation); a policy that expressly authorizes the category; a trigger that only lifts the cap on one defined obligation the represented party already controls. From the customer side, the same catalogue explains why a vendor will resist broad triggers; the customer's leverage is usually to name the specific obligations it cares about and attach them to the higher tier.

Counter by naming the obligation the counterparty is worried about and attaching it to the higher tier, not by trading the amount down. A lower cap and a narrower trigger are not interchangeable protections.

## Common positions
Negotiating context only, not the user's policy. Higher tiers are often keyed to data-protection, security or confidentiality breaches, sometimes all breaches of those sections and sometimes only breaches with a culpability qualifier. Gross negligence, willful misconduct and fraud are often uncapped or carved out entirely. General negligence and breach-of-law triggers are frequently proposed by customers and resisted by providers. Never present these as approved positions.

## Worked example
Packet: renewal; trailing fees $500,000. General cap = $500,000. Increased Claims cap stays 5.0x = $2,500,000 (policy permits up to 10x, within a $5,000,000 insurance limit; every category must tie to a defined obligation; any breach of §4 may qualify). Customer adds three categories: breach of §4 Security Incident notification obligations; "Provider's negligence"; "Provider's violation of applicable law". Policy: as stated.

Decision: the amount is unchanged and within policy; the triggers are the issue. Accept the notification category: it is a defined §4 obligation (72-hour notice) and within the any-§4-breach rule. Reject negligence: it can reach nearly every claim, raising the ceiling on routine claims from $500,000 to $2,500,000 ($2,000,000 more, against an insurance aggregate shared by all customers), and it overrides the gross-negligence qualifier on the existing §4 category. Reject applicable law: no laws or contractual duty are identified. Counter: any breach of §4 (dropping the gross-negligence qualifier, which subsumes the notification category) is an Increased Claim, offered as the answer to the customer's underlying data concern; ask what specific law-based concern exists and tie it to a defined obligation if one can be written. The answer reverses if the customer narrows "applicable law" to named laws attached to a defined compliance obligation, or if the policy authorizes negligence as a category.
