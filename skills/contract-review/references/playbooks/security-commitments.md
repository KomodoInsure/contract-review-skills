# Security commitments

Use when a customer proposes its own security schedule, addendum, questionnaire answers or control list, or edits the provider's security section: named controls, change restrictions, testing and report rights, and how the schedule is incorporated and ranked.

## Read first
- Each proposed control, one by one, against the security owner's written confirmation of what the provider does today, on which systems, and how it is evidenced (SOC 2 scope, pen-test scope, policies). Do not accept a control because it sounds standard; an unmet control is a breach on signature.
- How the schedule enters the contract: incorporated into the security section, attached as an exhibit, or referenced by URL; and the precedence clause. "Forms part of Section X and prevails over the Security Policy" means every line inherits whatever remedies attach to Section X and overrides the provider's own policy, including its change mechanism.
- What breach of the security section triggers: general cap, an increased-cap category (with or without a culpability qualifier), uncapped carveout, termination, audit rights. See [liability triggers](liability-triggers.md).
- Deployment: shared multi-tenant infrastructure versus dedicated hosting. A promise or test right that is harmless on a single-tenant instance can bind or endanger every customer on a shared one.
- The customer's driver: insurers, banks, health and public-sector customers often have vendor-oversight rules that require them to obtain specific controls or evidence. Identify the regime and what it actually requires of the customer before treating the ask as mandatory; research is usually needed.

## What drives the decision
Routine: specific, objective controls the provider meets today (encryption standards, MFA for administrative access, logging, background checks), written as outcomes with room for equivalent or stronger replacements; sharing existing independent reports (SOC 2, pen-test report or summary) under NDA on request; reasonable questionnaires; notice of material adverse changes.

Risk-changing: (1) change freezes or customer consent to modify controls. On a shared platform one customer's veto blocks patching, vendor replacement and upgrades for everyone; a frozen control list also ages badly (a named protocol version or algorithm can become the weak option). The usual answer is a no-material-reduction standard, not consent. (2) Customer-run penetration testing, scanning or inspection of shared systems: it risks other customers' data and availability and the provider's security programme; independent testing reports provide the assurance. (3) Report delivery terms beyond policy: full raw reports with unremediated findings, fixed short delivery windows, or unrestricted onward sharing. (4) Incorporation with precedence: converting each control into a numbered-section obligation that overrides the provider's policy turns ordinary control drift into a section breach, and, where the increased-cap category is any breach of that section, into a higher-tier claim. (5) Vague standards ("industry best practice", "all applicable frameworks") that make compliance unknowable.

Incorporation is often the decision point: accepting the controls the provider meets is easy, but the same list attached with "prevails" and no change mechanism carries the freeze and testing items into the liability scheme. Fix precedence by limiting the schedule to its listed controls and preserving the policy's update right; precedence of customer paper generally is covered in [customer paper and PO terms](customer-paper-and-po-terms.md).

Facts that reverse the answer: the security owner cannot confirm a control (do not accept it; offer a roadmap commitment only if authorized); dedicated hosting, where scoped customer testing under rules of engagement may be acceptable if policy allows; an applicable law or regulator rule that requires the customer to obtain a specific right (identify it and escalate rather than conceding by default); the represented party is the customer, where the same analysis argues for named controls, a no-material-reduction clause, report access and notice of material change rather than an unworkable freeze.

## Common positions
Negotiating context only, not the user's policy. Providers commonly commit to a written security policy or exhibit with a "no material reduction" change right; share SOC 2 Type II and pen-test reports or executive summaries under NDA; and refuse customer-run testing of multi-tenant systems, sometimes allowing it on dedicated environments with notice, scope and rules of engagement. Customers in regulated sectors commonly ask for named controls, report delivery, remediation commitments for critical findings and notice of material changes. Consent-to-change clauses are uncommon in multi-tenant SaaS and usually traded for notice.

## Worked example
Packet: new enterprise customer, a US insurance carrier, $450,000 annual fees. The customer's Information Security Schedule lists (a) AES-256 at rest and TLS 1.2+ in transit, (b) MFA for all administrative access, (c) delivery of the full annual pen-test report within 10 days of completion plus an annual customer-run pen test of the Service, (d) no modification of controls without Customer's prior written consent, (e) the Schedule forms part of §4 and prevails over the Security Policy. Standard §11(b) puts breaches of §4 resulting from gross negligence or willful misconduct at 5x fees. The security team confirms (a) and (b) are met today. Policy: agree specific controls met today; pen-test and SOC 2 reports under NDA; no customer-run testing of shared systems; Provider may update controls that do not materially reduce protection of Customer Protected Data.

Decision: accept (a) and (b) on substance, provided the Schedule keeps the update right (so a later move to stronger standards is not a breach). Split (c): offer the latest pen-test report under NDA on request after it is finalized, not a fixed 10-day delivery; reject customer testing of the multi-tenant Service and offer the third-party report, remediation status for material findings, or dedicated hosting at added cost (testing there only if policy is extended). Reject (d) and substitute the policy standard plus notice of material changes. Counter (e), which drives the decision: the Schedule may form part of §4 only as to (a) and (b), and the Security Policy, including its update right, governs everything else. As drafted, (c) and (d) would become §4 obligations: a single routine control change without consent is a §4 breach, at the $450,000 general cap or up to $2,250,000 (5 x $450,000) if found grossly negligent. Research flag: insurance-sector vendor-oversight rules (for example state insurance data security laws and, for New York licensees, 23 NYCRR 500) may require the carrier to obtain encryption, MFA and notice commitments from vendors; confirm what they require before treating (c) or (d) as mandatory, and escalate any genuine requirement rather than conceding. Reversal: if the security team could not confirm (a) or (b), or the deal had an any-breach-of-§4 trigger, the incorporation risk rises and (e) must be narrowed before anything else.
