# Data, confidentiality, security and AI uses

Map the data and purpose before interpreting a permission. Distinguish customer content, personal data, confidential information, feedback, usage/telemetry data, derived data and model outputs. Resolve exclusions between definitions: calling data “usage data” does not by itself remove personal-data or confidentiality obligations.

For each purpose (service delivery, troubleshooting, human review, security monitoring, analytics, model training/improvement, external disclosure), identify actor, data class, permission, restrictions, recipients, location and retention. A prohibition on training does not itself authorize inference, disclosure, logging or human access. Conversely, a training prohibition is not a prohibition on every AI use.

Check the relationship between the MSA, DPA, security exhibit, provider terms and order. Determine precedence within its actual scope. A DPA governs specified data-processing obligations; do not treat its existence as proof of every compliance requirement or unrestricted permission to transfer data.

Audit rights: separate who audits (the provider's independent auditor, the customer, a customer-chosen auditor, a regulator), what they may do (receive reports, send questionnaires, inspect, actively test systems), how often, at whose cost and under what confidentiality. A customer-run inspection or test of shared multi-tenant infrastructure can endanger other customers' data and the provider's own security programme. Where the provider's policy satisfies audit duties through independent certified audits, an auditor it selects, and reports shared under confidentiality, apply that position and offer its alternatives rather than drafting a guarded customer-audit clause the policy does not authorize. Check whether law actually applicable to these parties and data requires more (for example, a processor obligation or a regulator's access right); if it does, identify the gap and escalate rather than conceding by default. While escalating, still offer the measures the policy already allows (for example the latest independent audit report under NDA) so the counterparty has something to review in the meantime. Read frequency words carefully: "at least once every twelve months" sets a minimum, not a ceiling.

Distinguish contractual incident notice from statutory reporting. Identify event definition, awareness threshold, recipient, deadline, content and cooperation duties. Do not transplant a regulator's deadline into a processor-to-customer notice clause without checking roles and law.

Operational promises need evidence: deletion from active systems, backup expiry, restoration, legal hold, access restrictions, audit support and subprocessor changes. If actual capability is unknown, flag the commitment and the required owner confirmation. A retention exception should address permitted purpose, continued protection and eventual deletion, not create general reuse rights.

For required disclosure, check lawful compulsion, notice where permitted, scope minimization and cooperation. For representatives, check need-to-know, equivalent obligations and responsibility; permission to disclose does not necessarily make the representative a party.

AI-specific review should distinguish input rights, permitted processing, output use/ownership, accuracy promises, human oversight, prohibited use and IP allocation. Broad output warranties may be commercially significant; do not erase them solely because the service uses AI. Identify the actual capability, agreed performance and approved position.

Mandatory privacy/security requirements depend on jurisdiction, roles, data and use. Retrieve current authoritative guidance when that conclusion is requested. Do not conflate contractual permission with regulatory compliance.
