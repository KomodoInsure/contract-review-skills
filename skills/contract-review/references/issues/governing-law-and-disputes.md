# Governing law, venue and dispute resolution

Separate three switches: the law that interprets the contract, the forum that hears disputes (and whether it is exclusive), and the process (court, arbitration, jury waiver). A change to one does not change the others. Read the whole redline before closing the point; a counterparty's summary of "the main change" is not a compare.

## Weighing a proposed change
- Ask what the change actually costs the represented party: a forum it cannot practically litigate in, a legal system that would defeat a provision it relies on (cap, damages exclusion, warranty disclaimer, entire-agreement clause), or unfamiliar law with no predictable case law. A swap between well-developed commercial jurisdictions in the same country is often low-consequence; a foreign forum or law for the main agreement usually is not.
- Who is asking matters when the playbook ties legal redlines to a customer tier. A change acceptable for an enterprise deal may be outside policy for a customer on standard terms.
- Venue usually matters more than chosen law in practice. An exclusive forum in the counterparty's home courts, or abroad, is a separate concession from the choice of law.
- Check law-dependent provisions only where the conclusion depends on them, and research current authority rather than reciting rules from memory.

## Different law across one packet
Ancillary documents may lawfully carry their own law. A DPA or data-transfer clauses often must follow the law the data-protection regime requires, while the main agreement keeps its own law and forum. That split is not itself a defect: confirm that precedence and scope make clear which law governs which obligations, and that the main agreement's forum still controls commercial disputes. Do not recommend forcing every document onto one law unless the playbook or a real conflict requires it.
